## Article

The Spanish-born Antoni Gaudi's love of nature shaped his unique designs. Influenced by careful observation of plants and animals, he created curved buildings unlike any others.

Picture strolling through a park where benches curl like ocean waves, rooftops resemble dragon scales and stairways seem to float. You can do that in Barcelona, Spain, as you explore the strange and spectacular world of Antoni Gaudi.

Born in 1852 in Reus, a town in Catalonia, Gaudi experienced poor health as a child. Because he was frequently ill, he spent long periods observing plants, animals and shapes in nature. Many art historians believe this close attention to the natural world later influenced the curved lines and building forms he used. They look natural, as if they grew rather than were built, and are a defining feature of his designs.

Gaudi moved to Barcelona as a young man to study architecture and quickly became part of the city's artistic community. Unlike many architects of his time, he rejected straight lines and classical styles. Instead, he experimented with unusual shapes, colorful ceramic tiles and interesting forms, all of which were inspired by nature and religion. His work eventually became a major part of the Modernism movement in Catalonia, which is similar to art nouveau in the rest of Europe. Over time, his bold designs transformed Barcelona's skyline, attracting visitors from around the world and securing his place as one of the most original architects in history.

Visitors to Barcelona can see Gaudi's influence throughout the city. Park Guell, originally conceived as a residential development, is now a public park filled with bright mosaics, twisting benches and playful structures that seem almost alive. Casa Batllo is famous for its dragon-like roof and glittering colors. Nearby stands Casa Mila, also known as La Pedrera, with flowing stone walls and unusual iron balconies that seem to grow like vines.

One of Gaudi's most famous projects is La Sagrada Familia, a massive basilica in Barcelona. Construction began in 1882, and Gaudi worked on it for more than 40 years. The church is full of striking towers, stained glass and sculpted surfaces that tell biblical stories. Sadly, Gaudi never saw its completion. He died in 1926 after being struck by a streetcar.

Because the construction was so complex and because some of his original plans were destroyed during the Spanish Civil War, work has continued for decades. Current plans estimate that the basilica will finally be finished this year, one hundred years after Gaudi's death.

Today, Gaudi is considered not only one of Spain's greatest architects but also one of the most creative thinkers in the history of modern design. His legacy lives on in Barcelona, where the streets, parks and skyline reflect his remarkable imagination.
