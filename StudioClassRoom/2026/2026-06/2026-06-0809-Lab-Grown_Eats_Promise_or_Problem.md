# Lab-Grown Eats: Promise or Problem?

A closer look at the science and potential effects of lab-grown foods

by Christine Bedwell

## Article

The present rise in the world population and more frequent extreme weather events put global food security at risk. Produced from plant and animal stem cells grown in controlled environments, lab-grown food could provide sustainable, efficient and safe nutrition for a hungry planet.

To create lab-grown meat, scientists put animal cells into bioreactors, which act like an animal's body, providing heat and necessary nutrients. The multiplying cells develop into muscle and fat and are then shaped into types of meat like steak, boneless chicken or ground meat. For lab-grown dairy products, the genes for cow milk proteins are put into yeast, which converts the sugars into dairy proteins. These proteins are later combined with other ingredients to create a range of dairy products.

These innovative production procedures have the potential to improve animal well-being, increase food safety and nutrition and use fewer resources. A small sample of muscle tissue is taken from a living animal to produce lab-grown meat, saving the lives of animals. Additionally, lab-grown food is often safer because it is produced in a clean laboratory and can be engineered to include more nutrients than regular food.

Lab-grown food may also be more sustainable. Because it requires less space than traditional farming, water and land use are reduced. Additionally, bad weather, which can destroy crops and kill animals, doesn't affect lab-grown food.

Not everyone is convinced that lab-grown food is a good option. Presently, the taste and texture are not quite the same as traditional food. The U.S. company GOOD Meat mixes vegetable proteins with a small percent of lab-grown chicken to match the taste and texture of farm-raised chicken. Others note that because lab-grown foods have high energy demands, only renewable energy use will limit carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions. Lab-grown food costs more to grow, so only large-scale production will result in competitive prices.

Others hesitate because lab-grown meat tastes and feels unnatural and disconnects people from their food heritage. Italy became one of the first countries to ban lab-grown food to protect traditional food culture and domestic agriculture.

A variety of lab-grown foods, from meat and dairy to egg whites and tuna, are in production, some of which may appear in stores soon. But questions remain: Will lab-grown food prove sustainable and affordable in the future? And will the public accept this alternative nutrition source?

## Key Points

- Lab-grown food could improve food security while reducing resource use and harm to animals.
- Though it has great potential, challenges such as cost, energy use and taste still exist.
