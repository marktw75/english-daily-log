## Article

Visitors travel to eastern Canada every year to witness the massive blocks of ice that float by there. The largest blocks are tracked by satellites so ships can avoid them.

From April to August, people along the coasts of Newfoundland and Labrador in eastern Canada turn their eyes toward the Atlantic Ocean in anticipation of a remarkable spectacle. This region, often called Iceberg Alley, is one of the best places in the world to see icebergs drift by. Experts estimate that between 400 and 800 medium- and large-sized icebergs pass through Iceberg Alley every year. These towering blocks of ice travel south from Greenland, catching sunlight in shades of bright white, deep blue and even pale green. For many visitors, it feels like watching giant frozen mountains float by just offshore.

Iceberg Alley attracts so much attention because every iceberg is different. Some appear sharp and jagged, while others look smooth and rounded, like polished sculptures. Their shapes constantly change as seawater melts them.

One of the most intriguing features of icebergs is their sheer size. In 2017, a photo of an iceberg near the village of Ferryland, Newfoundland, went viral because the block of ice looked many times the size of the houses nearby. But only about one-tenth of an iceberg is visible above the water. Most of the iceberg is concealed under the water. The iceberg near Ferryland ran aground because that water was shallow compared to the iceberg.

Most icebergs start out as part of a glacier - a massive river of hard snow and ice that slowly slides toward the sea. When the front of a glacier reaches the ocean, parts of it crack and break apart. This natural process, called calving, sends enormous blocks of ice into the sea. Once a piece breaks off and begins to float, it becomes an iceberg. From there, ocean currents carry the iceberg south, often guiding it right into Iceberg Alley.

In 1912, an iceberg famously sank the Titanic about 400 miles south of Newfoundland in Iceberg Alley, so since 1914, organizations such as the International Ice Patrol have been monitoring them carefully. Now satellites track medium and large icebergs. Small icebergs, however, are none the less dangerous for their relatively small size. In fact, they pose the most danger to ships because they are too small to track via satellite and because they melt more quickly than larger ones, which makes their movement difficult to predict. As a result, the boat tours that go iceberg hunting always keep a safe distance, not only to avoid colliding with the icebergs but also because chunks of ice sometimes fall off the icebergs, creating waves.

Iceberg Alley offers a rare opportunity to witness an amazing natural process. For many travelers, seeing an iceberg in person becomes a memory they never forget.

## Types of Icebergs

- Tabular icebergs are short and wide with flat tops.
- Wedged icebergs have one side that goes up steeply and one that slopes more gently.
- Dry dock icebergs are shaped like the letter U.
- Dome icebergs are round on top.
- Pinnacle icebergs have at least one point, called a pinnacle, that sticks up.
- Blocky icebergs look like cut-off pyramids, with steep sides and sharp angles.
